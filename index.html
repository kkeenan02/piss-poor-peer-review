Submission cover letter

22 July 2026 · Kevin Keenan · 231 words

Not coded — see note at the foot of the page.

I am submitting a commentary, 'Objective, critical and pluralistic? Relationships and Sexuality Education in Northern Ireland after JR87 [2025] UKSC 40', for Commentaries and Notes. It runs to approximately 6,400 words including footnotes.

The piece takes up a question this journal has itself just raised. Deb, Graham and Murray's recent case note on JR87 (2026) 77 [journal] AD1 1 identifies RSE's parental opt-out as 'another area where a parental opt-out is becoming similarly politically charged', without pursuing it. My commentary is that pursuit: it applies the A2P1 objective, critical and pluralistic standard, which the Supreme Court has now confirmed is general and not confined to religious doctrine, to the RSE curriculum's group-identity-based content, and finds the same failure.

It also speaks to the journal's other live thread on definitional questions of sex and gender. I engage directly with Murray and O'Donoghue's commentary on For Women Scotland (77(1) [journal] 236), including where I think their argument is open to challenge, rather than leaving that disagreement to surface first in review.

I confirm the piece is not under review elsewhere, and am glad to anonymise it and supply contact details separately as your process requires.

P.S. Limited to this cover letter, I also offer a minor point of correction on Deb, Graham and Murray, which appears to cite 'Re JR87 [2022] NIQB 93', when it is in fact 'Re JR87 [2022] NIQB 53'.

02

Reviewer 1 — recommends decline

1 September 2026 · Reviewer 1 · 198 words

The paper presents a series of unevidenced yet sweeping claims about the RSE curriculum and its materials, none of which are made out on a perusal of those materials. These claims form the anchor for the author to substantiate the overall claim that the RSE curriculum is a breach of the ECHR for suffering from a lack of objectivity, pluralism and criticality. The author seems interested in attacking these unevidenced claims instead of engaging with the curriculum as it exists. The arguments presented are thus classic strawmen.

The author does not engage with any contrary viewpoint but dismisses and de-legitimises views and indeed entire disciplines on the basis of some non-specific 'ideology'. The resultant prose could use more measured language, which is a requirement for academic pieces. Dismissing entire disciplines is also unnecessary and distasteful. This kind of attack simply does not belong in an academic piece.

The few sources on which the author relies are imprecisely cited (e.g. the whole of the Cass Report is cited at various footnotes, instead of specific page references) and on at least one occassion, of poor quality. This lack of diligence and rigour does not commend itself to any academic forum.

03

Reviewer 2 — recommends decline

1 September 2026 · Reviewer 2 · 270 words

This submission engages with live and pressing societal issues and recent jurisprudence, but it takes these developments and spins them into conclusions that the core materials do not sustain. It falls someway short of the standard required for publication in [the journal]. First, the account of the relevant case law on presentation of contestable material in a curriculum as "objective truth" is truncated. It lacks an account of the limits of the ECHR jurisprudence or how the ECtHR's strictures are applied to comparable RSE provision in CoE states. The submission's application of these requirements of human rights law to the NI RSE syllabus is crude; there are some very brief and decontextualized excerpts from the syllabus which are not reflective of the syllabus as a whole. The assertions of the submission are ultimately incoherent; there is a recognition that sex and gender based analyses coexist within the syllabus which can scarcely be taken as a basis for the indoctrination of a particular position. Entire disciplines are, moreover, presented as having particular ideological bents, which does not reflect debate within these disciplines or how the overarching content is presented within the RSE syllabus. Several core propositions, such as the imposition of these rules from Westminster being objectionable in the post-conflict context of NI are not supported by any authority, calling the academic rigour of the work into question. For claims as far reaching as those made in this submission to be sustained, there would need to be a compelling account of the RSE syllabus in NI being far out of line with practice in other education systems subject to the ECHR.

Appeal

2 September 2026 · Kevin Keenan · 1,737 words

Not coded — see note at the foot of the page.

I am appealing the decision to decline my Commentaries and Notes submission, 'Objective, critical and pluralistic? Relationships and Sexuality Education in Northern Ireland after JR87 [2025] UKSC 40'. This appeal is made under the appeals policy published on the journal's publisher page ([url redacted]), as I could not locate any such policy on the journal's own site. That policy requires a significant factual error or major misunderstanding of the manuscript, or a compromised decision-making process. I raise this appeal on the 'major misunderstanding of the manuscript' grounds, and set out the evidence for that below, working through Reviewer 2 and Reviewer 1's comments emailed to me on Tuesday, 1st September.

The central misunderstanding

Reviewer 2 writes: 'The assertions of the submission are ultimately incoherent; there is a recognition that sex and gender based analyses coexist within the syllabus which can scarcely be taken as a basis for the indoctrination of a particular position.'

This is a literal inversion of my commentary's central argument, and a serious enough error to call into question whether the reviewer engaged with it at all. The reviewer treats the co-occurrence of two keywords in the syllabus as evidence the RSE content is unobjectionable. My commentary does something considerably more rigorous: it traces, strand by strand, how the curriculum's pedagogical sequence and content together fail the ECHR's OCP test. The entire 'Framework Applied: The Gender Content' section — nearly 30% of the manuscript by word count — sets out that content's logical incoherence in detail, citing the relevant jurisprudence throughout. I don't believe this misunderstanding reflects any incoherence in my own argument.

A standard that doesn't exist

Reviewer 2 closes with: 'For claims as far reaching as those made in this submission to be sustained, there would need to be a compelling account of the RSE syllabus in NI being far out of line with practice in other education systems subject to the ECHR.' That is not the legal standard, and the case at the centre of this submission proves it. The test I apply, set out early in the commentary, is content-internal: whether a state's own curriculum conveys information objectively, critically and pluralistically, not whether that curriculum is comparatively worse than curricula elsewhere in the Council of Europe. JR87 did not require any such comparative showing. The Supreme Court did not ask whether Northern Ireland's RE syllabus was unusually bad relative to RE curricula in other Council of Europe states before finding it in breach; it applied the Kjeldsen/Folgerø test to that syllabus on its own terms. If Reviewer 2's proposed threshold were the actual standard, JR87 itself would have failed for exactly the reason this review says my submission should. The reviewer is welcome to disagree with the Kjeldsen/Folgerø line of authority, but that is a disagreement with settled Strasbourg jurisprudence, not a defect in this manuscript. This is not a peripheral point: it is the standard the reviewer sets for whether any of the submission's claims can be sustained at all, and it is not the standard JR87 itself was decided under. Another complete misunderstanding of my commentary and its legal grounding.

Reviewer 2's other errors

Reviewer 2 opens by stating the manuscript 'spin [the case law and syllabus] into conclusions that the core materials do not sustain.' Given the misreadings demonstrated for this reviewer above, I don't think they are well positioned to make that judgement.

Having foreshadowed a general failure with 'It falls someway short of the standard required for publication in [the journal],' the review never demonstrates one. The only concrete claim offered is that I provide no 'account of the limits of the ECtHR jurisprudence,' which is simply false: I cite Lautsi for the limit on religious iconography and Kjeldsen for the same limit as applied specifically to sex education. The reviewer is right that I don't cite every relevant case, Dojan v Germany (2011) for example, but this is intentional, as a commentary is not a systematic review, and I made a deliberate choice not to aim for exhaustive coverage. Regardless, a missing citation or two cannot carry a judgement as sweeping as the one this review reaches about the manuscript's suitability for publication in [the journal].

The rest of Reviewer 2's comments follow the same pattern: a sweeping statement about the piece as a whole, backed by a narrow claim that is either wrong or, at best, a minor point. 'Brief and decontextualized excerpts... not reflective of the syllabus as a whole' is one example. I state explicitly, early in the commentary, what it is not about: the curriculum's empirically grounded content on reproduction, contraception and sexual health is not disputed and is set aside. I then analyse, at length, the specific group-identity content I argue fails the standard. That is not decontextualisation at all, but a commentary clearly stating its scope and making suitable arguments accordingly.

Reviewer 2 also writes: 'Entire disciplines are, moreover, presented as having particular ideological bents, which does not reflect debate within these disciplines or how the overarching content is presented within the RSE syllabus.' I do no such thing, and I'd ask the reviewer to identify the passage that does. My actual argument is narrower and methodological: a discipline's own self-described methodology constrains what claims drawn from it can be taught to children as objective fact, and content whose originating field describes its own propositions as interpretive and normative rather than measured fails that test. That is a claim about fitness for one specific use, not a verdict on any field's legitimacy. Conspicuously, reviewer 1 makes precisely the same error.

Reviewer 1's errors

Reviewer 1 takes the same mischaracterisation of my comments on the content's originating disciplines further, calling it 'an attack [that] simply does not belong in an academic piece.' It is a philosophical argument, not an attack, and a reviewer engaging with what I actually wrote, rather than a version they contrived, would have seen that. This is also the reviewer who calls my treatment of the syllabus 'classic strawmen' — an odd charge from a reviewer attempting to contend with an argument the commentary didn't make.

Reviewer 1 also writes that I 'do not engage with any contrary viewpoint.' I'd genuinely like to know what the reviewer thinks that would look like, given the commentary spends most of its length quoting the RSE curriculum itself, strand by strand, verbatim, with a footnote and access date for every quotation. The curriculum is the contrary viewpoint. Quoting it directly and showing where it contradicts itself, while attaching these failings to the ECHR's OCP test is exactly what a legal commentary should do. Why does Reviewer 1 accuse the commentary of not engaging with contrary viewpoint, when that is most of what the article is doing?

The same reviewer writes that none of my claims 'are made out on a perusal of those materials.' I'd suggest 'perusal' is doing the work in that sentence. A perusal is a skim. What I actually did was trace one pedagogical sequence in detail — LGBTQ+ Unit 4 sorts stereotyped traits by sex on a Venn diagram, asks what it must be like for a child who doesn't fit the pattern, then defines 'transgender' as exactly that non-correspondence and nothing else — and showed precisely where that sequence is circular and fails the OCP standard. If a perusal is genuinely what happened here, that would explain a great deal. Again, nothing substantive in the manuscript is engaged with by the reviewer on this point — just a repeat of the 'strawmen' charge.

That same reviewer's sentence, 'the author seems interested in attacking these unevidenced claims instead of engaging with the curriculum as it exists,' doesn't even parse. 'These unevidenced claims' refers back to the claims the review has just said the manuscript makes about the curriculum... so my own claims. Read literally, I am being accused of attacking my own claims, which isn't a coherent charge against anyone. If the reviewer meant I invented claims and attacked those instead of the curriculum's real content, that isn't what the sentence says, and it would be wrong anyway, since every claim under dispute is a direct quotation from CCEA's own materials.

One more point on this reviewer: they get from page-level imprecision in a citation for the 'Cass' Report, and a couple of sources covering events that were only weeks old at submission, all the way to 'does not commend itself to any academic forum.' I'd ask that conclusion be weighed against how this journal treats the same type of imprecision elsewhere. a case note on JR87 published in this same journal cites the first-instance decision as '[2022] NIQB 93' throughout; the correct citation, taken from the judgment's own neutral-citation header, is NIQB 53 — an error I flagged to you in good faith when I submitted my own article. That piece was published and stands. Minor citation imprecision evidently isn't disqualifying in this journal, and certainly isn't a disqualifier for participating in 'any academic forum'.

What I accept

I'm not appealing everything either reviewer wrote. The Cass Review is cited to the report as a whole rather than to specific pages at several footnotes, and I'll fix that. Footnote 25, as Reviewer 3 correctly flagged, was an unresolved drafting placeholder rather than a finished citation, and that's an easily fixed production issue, not a scholarly disqualifier. My paragraph on the syllabus's imposition in Northern Ireland's post-conflict context states its conclusion without spelling out the premise that gets you there, and I'll make that premise explicit. I'd push back gently on the reviewer's framing, though: it's a stated opinion in a commentary, not a factual claim, so faulting it for lacking 'any authority' is an odd standard to apply.

What's actually left

Strip away the mischaracterisations above and here is what remains: neither reviewer disputes that the curriculum's three gender definitions exist as quoted, or that they conflict. Neither offers an alternative, non-circular reading of the Venn diagram sequence. Neither touches the constitutive/discretionary norms distinction the whole argument is built on. Neither disputes my reading of Kjeldsen, Folgerø, Lautsi, or JR87 itself. What's actually being objected to is tone without compelling evidence, and citation completeness, not whether the central argument holds up, and I don't think a decline decision should rest on that.

I'd ask that this decision be reconsidered against what the manuscript actually says, not what these two reviews have mischaracterised it as saying. I'm glad to submit a revised manuscript fixing the points above that are genuinely fair.

Reply to appeal — decision final

7 September 2026 · Chief Editor · 222 words

Thanks for this.

The journal is not yet published by its incoming publisher (that is from January 2027 onwards), and as such the procedure you've found does not apply.

I appreciate you've put a lot of thought into your response to the decision. However, at this point the decision is final. There are serious issues with the submission, as identified by two peer reviewers, and it is not possible to take it forward. A third reviewer is more encouraging, but we cannot disregard the majority view.

Reviews can be difficult to accept. Anyone who has sought to publish in a law journal, or obtain a book contract, or read a review of their books, sometimes feels wronged in some significant way. I find - and advise - it is best to see the review process, in whatever form, as a dialogue.

Thinking about the reviews you've received as part of an dialogue, which you've continued via your email, may be most productive. Indeed, the journal remains open to a new submission that takes the reviews into account, but this would of course need to be a very significant revision (your thoughts along those lines already seem to be developing, but the decision on the piece remains final). You are also free to submit the submission or a different submission on the same topic elsewhere.

Thanks for this.

I can only offer the following by way of assistance.

In any review process, I've found it best to enter into my interlocutor's position as fully as possible. That involves setting aside efforts to defend my work such that I refuse any changes, and to think again, this time by coming at things from the interlocutor's point of view, and opening space to think and write about things differently. But to do this, I've found time and distance are helpful.

Having a piece declined, or any sort of negative reviews, can feel awful, especially if we've tried hard. Nevertheless, looking back, I've found, and continue to find, such resistance provides a way to improve and eventually succeed.

I hope this provides some help.
